Category A

Brand & first impression

What a visitor sees before they've read a single word — the split-second judgment that decides whether they keep scrolling. Everything in this category lives on the homepage, above the fold.

1. The split hero

/
What it is

A full-bleed image split down the middle: a dog on the left panel, a cat on the right, separated by one hairline seam. Both panels are exactly equal width and get identical treatment — same headline styling laid over both, same darkening overlay, same scale.

Why we need it

Most competitor sites in the research defaulted to a dog hero, with cats mentioned somewhere in a paragraph below the fold. A cat-owning visitor learns within one second whether they're a priority here or an afterthought — and a hero that's visually 50/50 answers that before a single sentence of copy has to argue the point.

From the homepage

This is the homepage. It's the very first thing that loads — nothing to click, just let it sit on screen for a second before you start talking.

2. Inline date-check bar

/
What it is

An arrive-date, leave-date, and guest-type field, built directly into the hero rather than living on a separate page. Filling it in and clicking through takes you straight into the Stay Planner with those dates already loaded.

Why we need it

The research flagged this repeatedly: sites that make a visitor hunt for a “check availability” link lose people before they ever see a price. Putting it in the hero turns the opening screen from a poster you look at into a tool you can actually use immediately.

From the homepage

Same screen as the hero — it's the light-colored bar sitting just below the headline. No scrolling required.

3. Logo & palette, sourced from real artwork

Header, footer, favicon
What it is

The site's navy-and-orange color system wasn't picked from a swatch book — it was extracted as real, measured hex values sampled directly from the commissioned mascot logo (a deep blue, a bright cyan, a golden yellow, and an orange). Every background, text color, and accent across all 18 pages derives from those four sampled colors.

Why we need it

A logo that doesn't match the site around it reads as two different projects stitched together. Deriving the whole palette from the logo's own colors means the header, the buttons, the dark sections, and the mark itself all read as one deliberate identity.

From the homepage

Top-left of the header — visible right now without clicking anything, and it stays in the same spot on every page you visit afterward.

4. The two-wing positioning line

/
What it is

The sentence directly under the hero headline states the business's literal structure: two wings of one suite, one for dogs and one for cats, each behind its own locked door with its own air handler.

Why we need it

It's not a marketing tagline layered on afterward — it's the actual architecture, stated plainly, and it previews the dual-species system that every other page on the site reinforces. Saying it first sets the expectation for everything that follows.

From the homepage

Same hero, the paragraph directly beneath the large headline text.

Category B

Trust & transparency

The research's single loudest finding: this category sells peace of mind, and the weakest competitor sites hide the exact information that would provide it.

5. Every rate published, in full

/rates
What it is

A single page listing every room rate, every add-on, the full grooming menu, and every shuttle fare as a real dollar figure — including the exact holiday surcharge windows, published a full calendar year in advance.

Why we need it

“Call for pricing” was the single most-cited failure pattern across the fifty competitor sites reviewed. Publishing every number removes the single biggest source of booking hesitation — nobody has to call, wait, or discover a markup once they're already committed.

From the homepage

Scroll to “Rates, plainly” (about three-quarters down the page) → click “Every rate, in full” — or just click Rates in the top navigation at any point.

6. Vaccine requirements, stated upfront

/new-guests, /safety
What it is

The exact vaccines required for both dogs and cats, listed in plain text before a visitor ever starts a booking — not buried in a policy PDF discovered after payment.

Why we need it

Stating requirements early qualifies serious visitors and signals the business takes health seriously. The alternative — surprising someone with a blocked booking after they've already invested ten minutes in the flow — is one of the most common reasons a booking gets abandoned.

From the homepage

Scroll to “Before your first stay” near the bottom of the page for the summary, or click New Guests in the top navigation for the full requirement list.

7. Care & Safety page

/safety
What it is

A dedicated page spelling out staffing ratios, staff certifications, and a room-by-room sanitation schedule — written as specifics rather than summarized as a badge graphic.

Why we need it

Any business can display a “certified” badge. Stating exactly what that means — which certification, held by whom, checked how often — is what actually reads as credible to a skeptical pet owner rather than decorative.

From the homepage

Click “The Inn” in the top navigation → “Care & Safety” in the dropdown.

8. Hurricane & heat protocol

/safety
What it is

A written hurricane-season protocol with a named inland evacuation destination, plus a separate summer heat protocol for Houston conditions — both grounded in the site's real Clear Lake, Houston address.

Why we need it

Clear Lake is genuine hurricane-surge territory. A generic “we have emergency procedures” line would have read as boilerplate; a real plan with a named destination reads as a business that has actually thought it through.

From the homepage

Click “The Inn” → “Care & Safety” — same page as above, scroll roughly halfway down.

9. Policies in plain language

/policies
What it is

Cancellation terms, medication handling, and behavior policy written in short, direct sentences rather than legal hedging.

Why we need it

The research found dense policy walls are one of the most common reasons a visitor abandons a booking mid-flow. Short, direct language keeps the trust without demanding a legal reading.

From the homepage

Not in the top navigation — scroll all the way to the footer → “Policies” under the “Practical” column.

Category C

The booking experience

The actual conversion path — and the one place the numbers have to be genuinely correct throughout, not just look correct on the final screen.

10. The Stay Planner, five steps

/plan
What it is

A guided flow — Guest, Dates, Room, Extras, Details — with a running price total visible in the sidebar from step one, not revealed only at the end.

Why we need it

The research repeatedly flagged multi-step booking flows that hide the total until the last screen as the single biggest driver of abandonment. Showing the number build as you go removes that anxiety entirely.

From the homepage

Click “Plan a Stay” — the dark button, top-right of the header, visible on every page including this one.

11. A real availability engine

/plan
What it is

Room availability is computed deterministically from the specific date and room — not randomized on each page load — so the same date always shows the same scarcity everywhere it's referenced.

Why we need it

A demo where the calendar, the room list, and the confirmation screen show three different numbers for the same date undermines the whole flow instantly. Consistency here is what makes the planner trustworthy to actually use live.

From the homepage

Plan a Stay → step 2, “Dates” — pick a date near a real holiday (Thanksgiving or Christmas week both work well) to see scarcity and surcharge appear together.

12. One pricing engine, no hardcoded numbers

/plan, /rates
What it is

The peak-night surcharge, the extra-animal fee, and the Quiet Wing surcharge are each defined exactly once in the codebase, and every page that quotes a price — the planner, the rates page, the room descriptions — reads from that same source.

Why we need it

It's built the way a real production system would be, specifically so a price can never quietly drift out of sync between two pages — the exact kind of inconsistency that makes a customer stop trusting a listed price.

From the homepage

Plan a Stay → pick a peak-week date — the surcharge line that appears will match the figure on the Rates page exactly, worth pointing out side by side.

13. Sold-out doesn't mean dead end

/plan
What it is

If every room is booked for the chosen dates, the planner proposes the nearest available window instead of just returning an empty result.

Why we need it

A flow that simply stops loses the customer. One that offers the next-best option keeps the conversation — and the booking — alive.

From the homepage

Plan a Stay → step 3, “Room” — if you land on a fully-booked date, the alternative-dates message appears here automatically. Not guaranteed on every date, so check step 2's scarcity indicators first if you want to land on one deliberately.

14. A real confirmation state

/plan
What it is

The flow ends on a reference number and a plain-language explanation of what happens next, rather than a vague “thank you” message.

Why we need it

It's what proves the flow was actually finished end-to-end during the build, rather than left half-implemented past the point where it stops being visible in a quick look.

From the homepage

Plan a Stay → complete all five steps → submit on “Details” to see it.

Category D

Dual-species equality

This is the site's central thesis, enforced structurally rather than just claimed in copy a later edit could quietly stop honoring.

15. Two Houses, one toggle

/
What it is

One room-index component serves both species — identical arch-shaped photo frame, identical four-tier layout, identical level of detail. Toggling to cats doesn't drop into a smaller secondary view; it swaps in the Cattery's own four rooms and its own photography.

Why we need it

Building one shared component that both species must fit equally into is a structural guarantee of parity — it's much harder to accidentally shortchange one species when they're rendered by the exact same code path.

From the homepage

Scroll past the opening “Promise” section to “Two Houses, one inn” — click the Dog/Cattery toggle at the top of that section.

16. Two genuinely different daily schedules

/ (A Day at the Inn)
What it is

An hour-by-hour timeline for a typical day, toggled between dog and cat — and the cat version isn't the dog version with nouns swapped. No group play at all; ten minutes of hunting-style play instead of an hour outside; a genuine four-hour untouched rest stretch in the middle of the day.

Why we need it

Writing a structurally different schedule — one that reflects how cats actually sleep and play differently from dogs — is what makes the equality claim credible instead of decorative. A copy-paste day-in-the-life would have been an obvious tell.

From the homepage

Keep scrolling past Two Houses to “A Day at the Inn” — use the same Dog/Cat toggle at the top.

17. The Cattery as its own world

/cattery
What it is

A dedicated page with cooler lighting, more air in the layout, and a slower pace than the dog-facing pages. The headline claim is stated literally: no dog has ever been inside the building, with a separate entrance and separate air handling as the very first line.

Why we need it

Cat owners specifically distrust facilities where “we take cats too” means a spare kennel down the same hallway as the dogs. Giving cats their own genuinely distinct visual world, not just a color swap, addresses that skepticism directly.

From the homepage

Click “Stays” in the top navigation → “The Cattery” under the Cats column.

18. An automated balance auditor

scripts/balance.mjs
What it is

A script that counts dog-versus-cat representation across the entire site — word counts, image counts, room counts, named-guest counts — and fails the build if either species drifts more than 25% ahead of the other.

Why we need it

Parity that's only asserted once tends to erode as a site grows and pages get added by different people at different times. Measuring it automatically on every change is what keeps the claim true over time instead of just true on launch day.

From the homepage

Not something to click to — this lives in the codebase, not the live site. Mention it verbally as the reason the balance you're showing across every other feature in this category actually holds up; showing it live would need the project open in a terminal, not the browser.

Category E

Competitive differentiators

Features almost none of the fifty researched competitors had — the reasons a visitor picks this business over the next tab.

19. The Quiet Wing

/quiet-wing
What it is

Six rooms behind a soundproofed door, for animals who can't handle a normal boarding day — reactive dogs, cats who've never boarded successfully anywhere, anyone medically fragile — open to both species equally, at a stated $18/night over the base room rate.

Why we need it

The surcharge is framed explicitly as the cost of the extra one-on-one staffing, not marked up as a premium tier that exploits anxious owners. That framing, plus offering it to cats as readily as dogs, is close to unique in the category based on the research.

From the homepage

Scroll past “Four ways to stay” to the dark full-bleed section — or click Stays → the featured Quiet Wing panel on the right side of that dropdown.

20. The Kitchen

/kitchen
What it is

A real cooked-to-order dining menu with separate dog and cat sections — but the page opens by stating plainly that their own food is prepared and served at no charge.

Why we need it

Leading with the free option is what makes the paid menu read as a genuine choice for a special occasion rather than a pressure upsell layered onto a boarding stay.

From the homepage

Click “The Inn” in the top navigation → “The Kitchen”.

21. The Airport Run

/shuttle
What it is

A scheduled pickup service priced and worded around the real geography of the address: Hobby is the close airport from Clear Lake (about 20 minutes), IAH is the long one (about 65), plus runs to League City, Friendswood, Pearland, and Nassau Bay/Seabrook.

Why we need it

A generic “we're near the airport” line wouldn't have survived contact with the actual address — writing the real distances made the feature specific and checkable rather than vague.

From the homepage

Click “The Inn” in the top navigation → “The Airport Run”.

22. The Den Cam

/den-cam
What it is

A camera-selection interface with a locked (signed-out) state and an open (signed-in) state, covering five feeds across both wings of the building.

Why we need it

The feeds are deliberately labeled as sample frames rather than presented as a live stream. Being upfront about what's simulated matters more than making a demo look more real than it is.

From the homepage

Click “The Inn” in the top navigation → “The Den Cam”. Try it signed out first, then sign into the Guest Portal and return to see the difference.

Category F

Guest relationship & retention

What keeps a customer coming back after the first stay — features aimed at the second, third, and tenth booking, not just the first one.

23. The Guest Portal

/portal
What it is

Signs in with any demo email and password into a full returning-customer dashboard: an upcoming stay, two pet profiles, an automatic vaccine-expiry warning, and complete report-card history.

Why we need it

This is what a fourth-stay customer's relationship with the business actually looks like — proving the site was designed for retention, not just a single first-time booking.

From the homepage

Click “Guest Portal” — top-right of the header, next to the Plan a Stay button. Sign in with anything; it's a demo login.

24. Report Cards

/ (While You Are Away), /portal
What it is

A real written daily note — meals, mood, a specific thing that happened that day — not a form of generic checkboxes.

Why we need it

The research called this out specifically: a templated report card reads as automated even when a real person filled it in. A written sentence about something specific reads as someone who was actually paying attention.

From the homepage

Scroll to “While you are away”, right after the daily timeline and the four-ways-to-stay panels. Full history is also visible inside the Guest Portal after signing in.

25. Guests of the Inn

/
What it is

A grid of nine named animals, each with one specific observed quirk rather than a generic “happy pets” caption.

Why we need it

Specificity is what makes it believable — a business that notices one particular animal's particular habit reads as a business that's genuinely watching, not just posting stock photography.

From the homepage

Keep scrolling past the Quiet Wing's dark section — it's the photo grid immediately after it.

26. Testimonials tied to real guests

/
What it is

Customer quotes that each reference the specific named animal and their stay history, cross-linked back to the Guests of the Inn section above.

Why we need it

An anonymous five-star blurb is easy to distrust. A quote tied to “Biscuit, 14 stays, the one who inspects every new arrival” is much harder to write off as fabricated filler.

From the homepage

Immediately below Guests of the Inn — same scroll, next section down.

Category G

Content realism

The difference between a site that feels like a real business and one that feels like a template with placeholder text swapped in.

27. Built from competitive research, not guesswork

Every page
What it is

The starting point for this entire project was a fifty-site research report ranking and analyzing real pet-boarding competitors — what the strongest sites do well, and exactly where the weaker ones consistently fail.

Why we need it

Nearly every feature in this catalog traces back to a specific finding in that report. It's the difference between a site built on opinion and one built on evidence of what actually works in this category.

From the homepage

Not a single page to click to — it's the throughline across the whole site. Worth saying out loud as context rather than pointing at; the original report itself lives in the project files if you want to reference it directly.

28. 66 curated, hand-reviewed photographs

Throughout
What it is

Every image on the site was sourced, cropped, and visually inspected before use. Several were rejected and re-shot after review caught a bad crop or an off-brand background — not just accepted on the first pass.

Why we need it

Generic stock photography is one of the fastest ways a site reads as a template. Hand-reviewed, consistently toned photography is what makes 18 different pages feel like one coherent place.

From the homepage

Best shown by scrolling through two or three pages back to back — the homepage, /suites, and /cattery all have strong examples. For the full photo credit list, scroll to the footer → “Unsplash contributors,” or go directly to /credits.

29. A real address, and content built around it

/the-inn, /contact
What it is

1051 Pineloch Dr, Clear Lake, Houston is a real commercial address. The property story, the room layout, and even the airport-distance copy in the Airport Run feature were rebuilt to match what's actually true about that specific location.

Why we need it

A placeholder city name is easy to spot. Content that's provably accurate for a real address — correct airport distances, a genuine hurricane-risk area — reads as researched rather than generated.

From the homepage

Click “The Inn” in the top navigation → “The Property” for the address-grounded story, or scroll to the footer on any page for the address and hours.

30. A fictional business, clearly disclosed

/credits, footer
What it is

All Paws Inn doesn't exist. That's stated in the footer of every single page, and in full detail on a dedicated credits page, alongside photo attribution for every image used.

Why we need it

Realistic content and honest disclosure aren't in tension — the site is written to feel real precisely because it's transparent about not being real, rather than trying to quietly pass itself off as an actual business.

From the homepage

Scroll to the very bottom of the footer for the one-line disclosure, or click through to /credits from that same line for the full version.

Category H

Engineering quality

What holds all of the above together under real use — the parts a visitor never sees directly but would notice immediately if they were missing.

31. Zero accessibility violations

All 18 routes
What it is

An automated accessibility audit (axe-core) runs against every page and currently reports zero violations, backed by manual keyboard-only testing on the planner, the navigation menu, and the calendar.

Why we need it

A booking site that only works with a mouse quietly excludes real customers — screen-reader users, keyboard-only users, anyone with a motor impairment. This isn't a compliance checkbox; it's real customers who can otherwise book with a competitor instead.

From the homepage

Not a page — a live way to show it is to press Tab repeatedly from the top of any page and watch the focus outline move logically through the header, the menu, and the content, without ever getting lost or stuck.

32. Verified responsive, not just “looks fine”

All pages
What it is

Every route is screenshotted and checked at five real breakpoints, from a small phone up to a wide desktop, with automated checks for horizontal overflow.

Why we need it

Horizontal overflow — a page that scrolls sideways by a few pixels on a phone — is a common, easy-to-miss bug on long pages with tables and photo grids, and it's the kind of thing that quietly makes a site feel broken to a mobile visitor.

From the homepage

Not a page — the simplest live demonstration is just resizing the browser window narrower while on any page and watching the layout adapt cleanly, particularly the header collapsing into the mobile menu.

33. 22 custom quality-assurance scripts

scripts/
What it is

Purpose-built tooling beyond generic testing: a keyboard-only audit, an edge-case audit covering sold-out dates and form validation and session persistence, a link crawler that checks every internal link resolves, and the dog/cat balance checker among them.

Why we need it

Quality was verified repeatedly through the build process, not checked once at the very end. That's what catches a regression — like a feature quietly breaking after a later, unrelated change — before it ships rather than after.

From the homepage

Not something to click to — these are development-time scripts, not part of the live site. Best mentioned as context for why the rest of the site holds together as well as it does.

34. Structured, typed content — not copy scattered in components

src/data/
What it is

Eleven typed data files hold every room, rate, guest, and policy as structured data that the pages read from, rather than text written directly and repeatedly inside individual page files.

Why we need it

It's the reason a rate can be changed in exactly one place and correctly update everywhere it's quoted across the site — the same architectural discipline that makes feature 12's pricing engine possible in the first place.

From the homepage

Not visible on the live site at all — it's the codebase structure underneath everything you've already shown. Worth one sentence at the very end of a walkthrough as the reason nothing you demonstrated ever contradicted itself.
